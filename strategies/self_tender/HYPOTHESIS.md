# Self-tender: a company that offers to buy back its own securities

Written 2026-11-17 23:35 ET, before anything is measured. A company that files an issuer tender offer
(SEC form SC TO-I) is offering to buy its own securities at a set price or a price range. For
its shares this is the strongest form of buyback: management names a price above the market
and commits cash. Published work (1980s to 2000s) finds the stock beats the market after the
offer closes. It is a long trade, and the form is public the moment it is accepted.

## Events

SEC filing times (the owner's download), form SC TO-I (the first filing, not amendments),
accepted before 2023-11-09, 2005 to 13 October 2023, by a filer whose SEC number Sharadar maps
to a stock in the price table. The first SC TO-I by that filer in 365 days (funds that tender
every quarter drop out). The stock must be common stock, $50M or more in market value, $250,000
a day or more, and $2 or more at the last close.

The form does not say what is being bought: most are offers for the common stock, but some are
for convertible notes or preferred shares, and some are offers to exchange employee options.
They are kept together; that mix is one reason a real effect could read weaker here.

## Trade

Bought at the first open after the SEC accepted the form (that morning's open if accepted
before 09:30 on a session day), held 20 or 60 sessions. Costs: the auction allowance and
commission. Measured against the average common stock of the same size ($50M–$300M, or $300M
and over) with $250,000 a day, bought at the same open and held as long.

## Cells

2 sizes × 2 holds = **4**, family `self_tender`.

## What counts

Search 2010–2017: 1% a trade over the average for 20 sessions, 2% for 60, weekly t of 3, six
years of eight, positive after costs; the count is reported. Tests on 2005–2009 and 2018–2022
at half those, t of 2.5, four years of five. 2023 is a check. The holdout stays sealed.
